Choosing an English to Japanese patent translator is not about finding someone who says yes to every question. It is about finding someone who can explain exactly how they would answer each one.

Most vendor evaluations for a Japan filing stop at credentials: years in business, a certification, a list of technical fields on a website. Credentials describe what a translator claims, not what a translator actually does when a technical field is unfamiliar, when the source document itself contains an error, when a claim term stays ambiguous, when a reference sign does not quite match the drawing, or when a quality process has never actually been tested.

The 5 questions below are the ones worth asking before you hire anyone, freelance translator or agency, to handle a Japan filing. Under each one is my own answer, drawn from the AI-assisted patent translation system I described in an earlier post.

## 1. How does your translator handle a technical field they have not worked in before?

Anyone can say yes when asked whether they have expertise in a given field. The more useful question is what happens when the honest answer is no, or only partially.

In my own system, every recurring technical term gets locked before translation starts. For a field I know well, medical devices or mechanical engineering, that lock draws on years of accumulated judgment calls, recorded in a rules file built from real projects rather than guesswork. For a field further from my core experience, the same locking process still runs: built from the client's own glossary when one is supplied, or from a first pass of dedicated terminology research when it is not. The workflow does not change. What changes is how much of the initial judgment is mine versus the client's.

A translator who claims universal expertise across every technical field is overselling, since no single person genuinely covers that range. A translator who can describe the process for handling an unfamiliar field, not just the claim of expertise, is the one whose answer is worth trusting.

## 2. What does your translator do when the source patent itself contains an error?

A translator's job is not to change the source document without saying so. It is also not to translate an error faithfully into the target language without comment. Both extremes fail the client by withholding something the client needs to know: a silent correction and a silent pass-through are the same failure wearing two different faces.

When my system or my own reading catches an inconsistency in the source, a reference sign that does not match the drawing, a term used 2 different ways, a claim element that drops out partway through the specification, the finding gets recorded in a translation note delivered with the project. The mechanical pass usually catches the simpler version of this first. The version that only shows up on a full read-through, a claim element that narrows in scope by page 40 with no single sentence announcing the change, is the reason I read the entire document before anything ships. The note states what was found and why it matters, then leaves the decision on how to proceed with the client and their counsel.

If your translator cannot describe how they surface a source error, one of two things is happening. Either they have not encountered one yet, unlikely on any filing of real length, or they are not reading closely enough to catch it.

## 3. How does your translator resolve referential ambiguity in claim language?

A claim that recites "a plurality of fastening members," then 3 paragraphs later refers back to "the aforementioned fastening member" in the singular, creates a genuine question: does the singular reference mean one specific member from the group, or the group as a whole? A mechanical translation carries that ambiguity straight into Japanese. A careless one picks a reading without saying so.

Resolving that ambiguity is a technical reading question, not a legal one, though the two are easy to blur. The answer depends on what the underlying disclosure actually described, read across the full specification and drawings, not on a rule a glossary can encode. Read one way, the claim covers a group. Read the other way, it covers one member. That difference changes what the claim actually protects, which is exactly why the final call belongs to the attorney handling the filing, not to the translator. My job is catching the ambiguity before a translation locks it into Japanese, then flagging it in the same kind of translation note described above, instead of resolving it on my own.

Ask your translator to walk through one real example of this kind of ambiguity and how they handled it. A generic answer means they have not thought about it. A specific one, like the example above, means they have.

## 4. How does your translator cross-check reference signs against the drawings?

Confirming that "12" in the text is the same "12" the figure shows is the minimum version of this check, and it is not enough on its own. A more reliable version traces what each labeled part actually does across the document: the movements and functions attributed to that part have to hold together as one coherent mechanism from the first mention to the last, not just match its number to a drawing.

When that sequence does not add up, the cause is either a mistranslation or an inconsistency already present in the source. Either cause tends to surface as a drawing or specification objection from the examiner, and occasionally a clarity rejection if the confusion is severe enough to affect how a claim term itself gets read.

I run this check by hand, on every project. It is one of the few failure points in mechanical and medical device patents where I have not found a version of automating it that I trust enough to remove myself from the loop.

If your translator describes the check only as matching numbers to a drawing, ask what happens when the numbers match but the described function does not.

## 5. What does your translator's quality process actually catch?

A translator who says a second linguist checks every project is describing a structure, not a result. What matters is what that check is designed to catch, and whether it catches the failures that actually cost a client trust.

My own process runs 2 automated passes before I read a single sentence: a mechanical check against every wrong rendering I have seen before, then an AI re-read that flags uncertain clauses by severity. Reviewing more than 10 million words of other translators' patent work made 3 failure types unmistakable, in the same order of severity: omission, where a word or clause drops out between source and target; inconsistency, where one source term gets rendered 2 different ways inside a single document; and differentiation failure, where 2 distinct source terms collapse into a single translation and lose the distinction the claim depends on. The system is built to catch all 3 automatically. What survives both passes and reaches me is the ambiguity no glossary entry or rule file could have caught in advance, the kind covered in questions 3 and 4 above.

A second linguist can catch a typo. A claim-scope problem is a different kind of failure, and whether the process catches that kind is the question worth asking.

## What the answers actually tell you

None of these 5 questions has a single correct answer. What they reveal is whether the person or team on the other end has thought carefully about where a translation can fail, and built something specific to catch each failure before the client does.
